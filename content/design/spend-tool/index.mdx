<StorySection>

<StoryChapter number="01 · Context and framing" title="Why spend management—and why the admin side." />

Spend management products have a well-documented employee-experience problem: most attention goes to expense submission. The admin side is consistently underserved, even though that is where financial control lives.

The African B2B context sharpens the problem. Corporate-card penetration is lower, teams often share a single card, and the resulting trust, fraud, and control implications are real. SpendTool explores an admin experience from the ground up—mobile first and grounded in that operational context.

<StoryPrinciples items={[
  { title: 'Reimbursement model', description: 'The employee spends first, submits later, and policy violations surface after the fact. Control is retrospective.' },
  { title: 'Card-first model', description: 'Limits, restrictions, and budget assignments are configured before a card is used. The card becomes the policy layer.' },
]} />

<StoryQuote>
  Setup thinking stays on the web. Mobile exists for the moment something needs the administrator’s attention or action right now.
</StoryQuote>

</StorySection>

<StorySection>

<StoryChapter number="02 · Product understanding" title="Requests and approvals look similar but operate at different levels." />

Treating Requests and Approvals as interchangeable creates an information architecture that confuses both the administrator and the product logic.

<StoryPrinciples items={[
  { title: 'Requests · Operational', description: 'Individual, time-stamped spend events tied to an amount and a person. This is an inbox—urgent and time-sensitive.' },
  { title: 'Approvals · Structural', description: 'The engine that determines who approves what, escalation triggers, and sign-off rules. It is configuration, not an inbox.' },
]} />

Requests therefore earn a permanent bottom-navigation destination. Approval logic appears inside each request as context—such as “Triggered by: Spend above ₦50k rule”—while full workflow configuration remains on the web.

</StorySection>

<StorySection>

<StoryChapter number="03 · Navigation architecture" title="Four tabs, each answering one administrator question." />

<StorySplit src="/images/Spend-tool/overview-screen-updated.png" alt="Question-led product model for Home, Requests, Tools, and Analysis" width={1122} height={1976}>
  <h3>A question-led architecture</h3>

  1. **Home:** Is everything okay with our money?
  2. **Requests:** Does something need my decision right now?
  3. **Tools:** Do I need to act on something specific?
  4. **Analysis:** How have we been spending?

  Urgency determines the order. Financial health and decisions come first; reflective reporting comes last.
</StorySplit>

<StoryPrinciples items={[
  { title: 'Home', description: 'Wallet balance, budget utilisation, and pending work provide financial health in under five seconds.' },
  { title: 'Requests', description: 'A transaction inbox that stays one tap away and explains which policy triggered each item.' },
  { title: 'Tools', description: 'Cards and Budgets get complete mobile actions; Team is limited; setup-heavy Approval configuration stays on web.' },
  { title: 'Analysis', description: 'Trend data is valuable but less urgent, so it occupies the final navigation position.' },
]} />

</StorySection>

<StorySection>

<StoryChapter number="04 · Home screen" title="The financial-health view answers one question: is everything okay?" />

<StorySplit src="/images/Spend-tool/homescreen.png" alt="SpendTool administrator home screen showing wallet, budgets, and pending requests" width={1140} height={2014} reverse>
  <h3>Designed for a five-second read</h3>

  - **Wallet balance:** The complete figure is shown while typical balances permit it; abbreviation is reserved for larger scales.
  - **Budget health:** Department budgets use utilisation bars so financial pressure is visible without another tap.
  - **Pending scope:** The request count appears in both the section heading and navigation state.
  - **No card carousel:** Individual cards belong in the Cards module. Home represents organisational health, not inventory.
</StorySplit>

<StoryChapter number="07 · Card issuance" title="One action becomes five isolated decisions." />

Issuing a company card combines identity, card type, budget, spend limits, and final verification. Compressing these decisions into a single form would increase cognitive load and make costly mistakes easier.

<StoryPrinciples items={[
  { title: '01 · Assignee', description: 'Search and filter the team rather than relying on a list that fails as the organisation grows.' },
  { title: '02 · Card type', description: 'Choose virtual or physical with operational differences explained at the decision point.' },
  { title: '03 · Budget', description: 'Assign the card while seeing each budget’s remaining capacity.' },
  { title: '04 · Limits', description: 'Set monthly and per-transaction controls within the available budget balance.' },
  { title: '05 · Review', description: 'Confirm the complete policy package before issuing the card.' },
]} />

<StoryChapter number="08 · Card controls" title="Freeze and deactivate share a row—but carry different weight." />

Freeze is a reversible state change; deactivate is permanent. Their interaction patterns need to communicate that difference before the administrator acts.

<StoryVisual src="/images/Spend-tool/card states.png" alt="Active and frozen SpendTool card states shown side by side" width={2512} height={2094} caption="The card visual and primary action both reflect its current state" wide />

<StoryPrinciples items={[
  { title: 'State is visible', description: 'The frozen card is muted and the primary action changes from Freeze to Unfreeze.' },
  { title: 'Language predicts outcome', description: 'Action labels describe exactly what will happen instead of relying on generic confirmation copy.' },
  { title: 'Permanent action gets friction', description: 'Deactivation names the cardholder, surfaces consequences, and requires a deliberate second step.' },
]} />

<StoryVisual src="/images/Spend-tool/card-deactivate.png" alt="SpendTool permanent card deactivation confirmation" width={2512} height={2200} caption="Two-step friction is intentional when an action cannot be reversed" wide />

</StorySection>

<StoryChapter number="09 · Budgets" title="The wallet is the ceiling; budgets are named envelopes." />

The wallet establishes the available pool before the administrator considers departmental allocations. Budgets then show usage, remaining value, assigned cards, and paused states as parts of that larger financial system.

<StoryVisual src="/images/Spend-tool/budgets.png" alt="SpendTool budgets list and budget top-up flow" width={1900} height={2078} caption="Top-up previews the movement from wallet balance to a named budget" wide />

<StoryPrinciples items={[

  { title: 'Paused means paused', description: 'A dimmed budget and explicit badge communicate inactivity before any label is read.' },

<StoryChapter number="10 · Team" title="Deactivating a member freezes their card—but does not destroy it." />

<StorySplit src="/images/Spend-tool/members-deactivate.png" alt="SpendTool team list showing active and deactivated members" width={1122} height={2040}>
  <h3>Separate the person from the financial instrument</h3>

  Deactivated members become visually distinct through reduced emphasis and status treatment. Member detail exposes linked cards and their current states before an administrator makes another decision.

  Deactivating the person freezes their card rather than permanently cancelling it. Permanent card deactivation remains a separate Cards-module action, keeping two different levels of consequence cleanly separated.
</StorySplit>

<StorySection>

<StoryChapter number="11 · Reflection" title="The most consequential decisions were structural, not visual." />

The work was shaped by questions that precede interface styling: what belongs on mobile versus web, how requests differ from approval rules, and where friction should be removed or deliberately added.

<StoryPrinciples items={[
  { title: 'Role context shapes everything', description: 'The administrator and employee experiences of the same system are fundamentally different products.' },
  { title: 'Mobile forces honesty', description: 'Every feature must justify its presence against an urgent question. If it cannot, it belongs on the web.' },
  { title: 'Friction is a design tool', description: 'Knowing where to add resistance—and where to remove it—is essential in financial product design.' },
]} />

<StoryQuote>
  The best fintech UX is not invisible. It is legible—the administrator always knows what state things are in, what they can do, and what will happen when they do it.
</StoryQuote>

Natural extensions include the employee-side mobile experience, a deeper Analysis module, and the full web counterpart where the configuration deliberately excluded from mobile would live.

</StorySection>
